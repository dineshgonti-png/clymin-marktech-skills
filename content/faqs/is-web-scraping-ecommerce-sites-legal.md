Web scraping publicly visible ecommerce data — competitor product prices, descriptions, and availability — is generally legal in the US under established case law, provided extraction is limited to publicly accessible data without authentication bypass and conducted with practices that avoid service disruption. ## The Legal Foundation for Ecommerce Web Scraping

The key legal precedent governing ecommerce web scraping in the US is the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals' ruling in hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn Corporation (2022). The court held that collecting publicly available data from websites does not constitute unauthorized access under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA).

The ruling's reasoning: publicly accessible data — information any user can see without logging in — is not "protected" from access in the CFAA sense. A competitor's product prices visible to any shopper browsing their website have been made public by the competitor. Collecting that public data systematically does not transform the collection into unauthorized computer access.

This precedent applies directly to the most common ecommerce competitive intelligence use cases: collecting publicly visible product prices from competitor websites, extracting product descriptions and specifications that any customer can read, monitoring publicly displayed availability and stock status.

According to the Electronic Frontier Foundation (2022), the hiQ ruling represents an important protection for competitive research, academic analysis, and journalism that depends on accessing publicly available web data systematically.

## What Data You Can and Cannot Collect

**Generally permissible (public data)**:
- Product prices displayed to any visitor without login
- Product titles, descriptions, and specifications
- Customer review scores and review text
- Availability and stock status
- Category structure and product organization
- Publicly advertised promotional pricing

**Generally not permissible (protected data)**:
- Account-specific pricing requiring login to access
- Exclusive trade pricing behind authentication barriers
- Private customer data
- Internal business data accessible through authentication bypass
- Data accessed by circumventing technical access controls beyond navigating anti-bot measures

Clymin's ecommerce data collection is limited exclusively to the publicly permissible category.

## Terms of Service: Limitations and Practical Significance

Amazon, Walmart, Shopify, and most ecommerce platforms include terms of service provisions restricting automated data collection. Amazon's ToS explicitly prohibits scraping. Shopify's ToS restricts systematic data extraction from merchant stores.

The legal enforceability of these provisions against general public data collection is limited following hiQ v. LinkedIn — courts have consistently distinguished between ToS as legitimate contract terms for platform users versus attempts to use ToS to prohibit collection of publicly accessible data by third parties.

However, ToS provisions retain practical significance in several ways: they establish the platform's stated policies, they may be relevant in jurisdictions where ToS has different legal treatment than under US law, and they create commercial relationship risk for businesses that are also platform partners (hotels that are both Booking.com partners and competitors monitoring Booking.com data, for example).

Clymin recommends that clients with specific concerns about ToS implications consult with legal counsel familiar with current web scraping precedents in their jurisdiction.
